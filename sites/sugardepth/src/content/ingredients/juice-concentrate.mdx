## What it is

Juice concentrate is fruit or vegetable juice with most of the water removed. What's left is mostly the fruit's sugar, which is why it shows up in "no sugar added" style marketing for snacks, yogurts and bars.

## How US labels count it

FDA's definition of added sugars includes sugars from concentrated fruit or vegetable juices that are **in excess of what would be expected from the same volume of 100 percent juice** of the same type. FDA also lists exceptions: juice concentrated from 100% juice and sold to consumers (like frozen orange juice concentrate), concentrates used toward a product's total juice percentage, and the fruit component of jams, jellies and preserves made under their standards.

FDA does not count single-strength juice, fruit purees (including concentrated purees) or fruit pastes as added sugar.

## Sugar Depth's verdict

| Printed on the label | Sugar Depth |
|---|---|
| Apple juice concentrate, concentrated grape juice, fruit juice concentrate | **UGLY** (rule U1, added sugar) |
| Apple juice from concentrate | Unresolved (rule A6). Reconstituted to normal strength it is not added sugar; used to sweeten another food it is. The label alone does not say which. |
| Fruit puree concentrate | Unresolved (rule A6). The label does not say how it was made. |
| Apple juice, orange juice (single strength) | Allowed in CLEAN |
| Fruit puree, applesauce, date paste | Allowed in CLEAN |

Sugar Depth matches juice wording with a small fixed grammar, so "apple juice concentrate" and "concentrated white grape juice" are both recognized without guessing.

Whether a 100% juice made from concentrate should ever be CLEAN is still an open question in Sugar Depth's rules. Today it comes back unresolved.
