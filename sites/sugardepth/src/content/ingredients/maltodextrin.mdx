## What it is

Federal regulations define maltodextrin as a **nonsweet** nutritive saccharide polymer made of glucose units, with a dextrose equivalent (DE) under 20, made by partially breaking down corn, potato or rice starch ([21 CFR 184.1444](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-184/subpart-B/section-184.1444)). It is used as a filler, thickener or carrier, for example in powdered mixes and tabletop sweetener packets.

## Not the same as dextrose, or resistant maltodextrin

- **Dextrose** is glucose, a sugar. It is an added sugar and makes a label UGLY. See [hidden sugar names](/hidden-sugar-names).
- **Resistant maltodextrin** (also listed as resistant dextrin, soluble corn fiber or wheat dextrin) is treated by FDA as a dietary fiber. It is also allowed in CLEAN.

Sugar Depth matches whole ingredient names exactly, so "maltodextrin" never matches "resistant maltodextrin" and "dextrin" never matches "dextrose".

## How US labels count it

Maltodextrin is a carbohydrate but not a sugar, so it is not counted in Total Sugars or Added Sugars.

## Sugar Depth's verdict: allowed in CLEAN

Maltodextrin does not trigger any rule. A label with maltodextrin and nothing sweet can be CLEAN (rule C1). The guide exists because the word sounds like sugar. Watch what it travels with: tabletop packets often pair it with [sucralose](/ingredients/sucralose), which makes the label UGLY.
